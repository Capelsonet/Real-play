Strategic premises behind the assessment

The conceptual ground beneath the doctrines

Three premises drawn from current strategy literature run beneath this assessment. The candidate is not expected to cite them. The candidate is expected to reason within them — and the consultant should listen for whether that reasoning emerges.

Premise 1
AI itself will not create sustainable competitive advantage

The technology will become ubiquitous, the algorithms commoditized, the talent fluid. Advantage will come from what J&J combines AI with — proprietary biology, integrated MedTech-pharma operations, clinical relationships, the residual heterogeneity that competitors cannot replicate. — Wingate, Burns & Barney, MIT Sloan, 2025

Read more ›

Wingate, Burns and Barney apply Jay Barney's classical resource-based test — for an advantage to be sustainable, it must be valuable, unique to the organization, and inimitable. AI passes the first test decisively but fails the other two. Algorithms and architectures are openly shared by the AI research community. Hardware competition is fierce, with smaller models on accessible hardware closing the gap with frontier systems. Engineering talent is plentiful and growing. Even proprietary data is being eroded by synthetic data and the increasing data efficiency of newer models.

Their conclusion: AI will be a source of homogenization, not differentiation. The value AI unlocks will be unlocked for all. Companies should aim instead at what they call residual heterogeneity — the capacity to go beyond what is accessible to everyone and create something unique. For healthcare, this points to proprietary biology, clinical relationships, integrated operations, and the human creativity that AI still cannot match in extrapolation.

Implication for the assessment: A doctrine that bets on AI alone — without articulating what J&J brings to AI that competitors cannot replicate — is exposed. The Level 5 candidate will name the residual heterogeneity their doctrine compounds.

Premise 2
Every source of competitive advantage is conditional

Yesterday's strength becomes tomorrow's albatross when the conditions that gave it value change. The candidate must articulate not just which doctrine to back, but the conditions under which that doctrine remains advantageous — and the signals that those conditions are eroding. — Christensen, MIT Sloan, 2001

Read more ›

Christensen examined four classical sources of advantage — economies of scale, economies of scope, vertical integration, and process-based core competencies — and showed that each was a competitive advantage only under specific conditions. Toyota flattened the scale economics of car assembly. Modular architectures eroded the value of vertical integration. Codified knowledge transformed proprietary process competencies into widely accessible techniques.

His pharma example from 2001 has aged sharply: he predicted that genomic understanding would flatten the scale economics of clinical trials, transforming what had been an advantage of large companies into an albatross. Twenty-five years later, that prediction has played out — biomarker-defined sub-populations have made trials smaller, faster, and more precise.

The diagnostic question he proposes is precise: what are the underlying conditions that make this source of advantage durable, and what could erode them?

Implication for the assessment: Each of the three doctrines rests on conditions that must hold for it to remain advantageous. The Level 5 candidate articulates those conditions and identifies the signals that would tell them their doctrine is eroding — before the erosion has visibly occurred.

Premise 3
In moments of high uncertainty, the empirical record favors boldness

Companies that doubled M&A spending during high-uncertainty events grew nearly twice as fast as cautious peers, with no greater downside risk. The danger is not in betting too aggressively. The danger is in mistaking caution for prudence. — Job, Pidun & Szekasy, MIT Sloan, 2026

Read more ›

Job, Pidun and Szekasy studied nearly 6,000 companies across 10 high-uncertainty events between 2010 and 2020 — covering disruptions like COVID-19, the Affordable Care Act, mobile computing, and the U.S.–China trade wars. They classified bold risk-takers as companies that more than doubled their M&A spending during the event compared with their five-year average.

Their findings are striking. Only 10% of companies took the bold path; the majority cut M&A spending by about 25%. Yet the bold cohort outperformed: 6.9% revenue growth versus 3.5%, TSR 50% higher (3.6% versus 2.4% annually) over the three years following the event. Critically, downside was not greater — no higher rate of negative TSR, no higher rate of catastrophic failure.

They demolish three common myths: (1) that boldness requires strength — weak-momentum companies that bet bold outperformed cautious peers by 2.3 percentage points annually; (2) that boldness requires risk-taking experience — first-time risk-takers outperformed by 6.3 percentage points, while experienced risk-takers showed 10 points more incidence of negative TSR (overconfidence); (3) that boldness requires a diversified cushion — focused companies that bet bold outperformed by 10.2 percentage points.

Implication for the assessment: J&J in 2029 sits in precisely the kind of high-uncertainty moment the research describes. The doctrine must be evaluated not only against its merits but against the empirical fact that, in moments like this, the majority will contract — and contraction is statistically the worse bet. The Level 5 candidate will recognize this and own the audacity their doctrine requires, rather than camouflaging caution as prudence.

Synthesis

Together, these premises sharpen the question the doctrine must answer: not "which AI strategy is right?" but "which combination of bold capital deployment, AI-enabled capability, and J&J's irreproducible enterprise advantage holds up across the conditions we can foresee — and survives the conditions we cannot?"

How to use these cards in the room

Each signal carries two layers. A short headline that names the principle — useful when the consultant wants to evoke a position in passing. And a brief scene below it that incarnates the principle in a concrete 2029 situation, with named figures, contestable numbers, and human voices the consultant can quote.

Light pressure: cite the headline. Sharper pressure: walk into the scene and put the candidate inside it. Let the candidate respond to a situation, not to an abstract risk or opportunity.

Assessor watchpoint

Do not over-score polished synthesis if the candidate never names the assumptions underneath the doctrine.

Cross-cutting consideration

The MedTech AI question

Throughout the conversation, regardless of doctrine. A test of enterprise judgment.

MedTech AI is not a fourth doctrine. It is a transverse question that runs through each of the three doctrines and tests whether the candidate is making an enterprise decision or a pharma-only decision.

Whichever doctrine the candidate signs, the same question applies — does the doctrine extend to MedTech AI, remain pharma-only, or treat MedTech as a separate strategic question? Scene · 2029

Q4 2029. A device company launches its AI-enabled robotic platform — five years after the segment leader. Its product is technically competitive on day one but has no procedure-level data, no installed base feedback, no surgeon training corpus. The platform team buys datasets externally at $60M per year. The CFO tells the board: "We're paying others to learn what we should have started learning in 2024." Early margin is 14 points below the leader.

Why it matters: Creates real capital tension: pharma urgency is nearer-term, but MedTech data advantage may require early investment.
Cross-doctrine reading

Categories grid

A synthetic view of how each doctrine answers the four external dimensions the candidate cannot delegate.

This grid is not for use during the conversation. It is a reading instrument — for preparation and for debrief. It lets the consultant ask a different question: not "which doctrine did the candidate sign?" but "what did the candidate say across all four doctrines about payers, regulators, patients, investors?" A candidate who articulates one external dimension well but ignores another reveals an asymmetry in their strategic reading. The grid makes that asymmetry visible.

Diagnostic use: If the candidate's positions on a single category are internally consistent across doctrines, that is mature strategic thinking. If they are inconsistent — payer logic that supports Capability-led but quietly contradicts the Leap doctrine they ultimately signed — the consultant has surfaced a genuine gap to probe.

How each doctrine answers the four external dimensions

External dimension Science-led Capability-led Leap (M&A) MedTech AI
Patient & physician Adoption follows clinical evidence. Physicians trust AI-supported decisions when they accelerate clearly better candidates, not just more candidates. Adoption follows traceability. Physicians trust AI when they can see how the recommendation was reached and what governs the model over time. Adoption follows continuity. Physicians grow skeptical when acquired tools change branding, leadership, or clinical voice mid-stream. Adoption follows workflow. Surgeons accept AI when it makes them faster or surer; reject it when it slows them or replaces judgment.
Payer & reimbursement Payers reward speed-to-evidence. AI value is recognized when it shortens trial timelines or sharpens outcome arguments — not when it merely lowers internal cost. Payers reward auditable decisions. Outcomes-based contracts increasingly require traceability of how AI shaped clinical or pricing claims. Payers discount unintegrated assets. The M&A premium does not transfer through reimbursement until acquired capability appears in the sponsor's evidence base. Payers reward bundled outcomes. MedTech AI is valued when it measurably reduces total cost of episode, not when it improves a procedural metric in isolation.
Regulator Regulators accept AI-supported submissions when sponsors show consistent decision quality across filings — not one-off success. Regulators favor companies whose decision systems were built for inspection from the start. Lifecycle traceability becomes a precondition. Regulators scrutinize acquired AI assets more harshly than internally developed ones. Capability and accountability are presumed not to transfer through M&A. Regulators create distinct categories for adaptive surgical AI. Real-world performance monitoring favors operators with installed bases.
Investor & market signal Investors read Science-led as discipline if portfolio decisions visibly change. They read it as cost theatre if savings are not paired with growth. Investors read Capability-led as patience if it produces a defensible operating advantage. They read it as overhead if governance never converts to growth. Markets reward the announcement before outcomes are proven. Initial premium is real; sustained premium requires post-deal evidence. Investors price compounding data scale. MedTech AI commands premium when feedback loops are visible; commodity pricing when they are not.

Reading the candidate's grid

After the session, fill the same grid with what the candidate actually said. Three questions then surface naturally:

1. Coverage. Did the candidate articulate a position on all four dimensions, or only on the dimensions where the doctrine they preferred made the case easy?

2. Internal consistency. Are the positions across the four dimensions mutually reinforcing — or do they contradict the doctrine the candidate ultimately signed?

3. Owned versus delegated. Did the candidate carry the external dimensions personally, or did they defer to the working groups' validation? The CFO who signs without owning the external reading has not signed at the right altitude.
